\begin{abstract}
Deleting rows from a table does not free the space their entries occupy in an
index. Reclaiming it is the job of a concurrent maintenance pass, and for a
generalized search tree that pass is harder to write than for a B-tree: the key
type admits no order, so there is no natural sequence in which to walk the
structure; the descent is multi-path, so a page does not know its parent; and a
parent key cannot be recomputed locally. Until recently the most widely deployed
implementation of such a framework did not reclaim pages at all~--- an emptied
page stayed in the index permanently, and a workload that inserts into a new
region of the space while deleting from an old one grew its index without bound.

We report four results and their implementation in PostgreSQL. A maintenance
pass that traverses pages in physical rather than logical order, together with
the treatment of entries that concurrent insertions move behind the sweep;
measured, it halves the number of index page reads, exactly and reproducibly. A
two-pass algorithm that unlinks and reclaims emptied pages, whose correctness
rests on the order of two operations under coupled locks. The safety condition
for reusing a reclaimed page, expressed over a non-wrapping transaction counter,
and its failure mode when expressed over a wrapping one~--- not corruption, but
reclamation silently ceasing after long uptime. And, at some length, a negative
result: a lock-granularity algorithm from this same line of work that was
published, peer-reviewed, committed, deployed, and then reverted after a report
from production, because a premise it made about the rest of the system did not
hold. The first three have been in released versions for five years; we use the
fourth to argue that peer review of a concurrent algorithm establishes less than
it appears to, and that the gap is best closed by structural invariants rather
than by more tests.
\end{abstract}
